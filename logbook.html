2  The Reading of Callow

By midnight, the easterly gale had arrived, not with howling squalls, but with a rhythmic, pulsing percussion against the basalt foundations. Every wave sent a dull vibration through the flagstones, into the legs of the iron chair, and up Julian’s spine.

He opened Thomas Callow’s daybook.

The early entries from October were precise, written in the cramped, angular hand of an experienced mariner:

Oct 12. Wind WNW force 3. Visibility 6 miles. Cleaned lamp prisms of brine crust. Replaced lead gasket on freshwater cistern valve. Three fishing smacks rounded the north reef at dawn.

Oct 29. Spring tides higher than chart datum by eleven inches. Water reached third rung of gallery ladder at noon slack. Water remarkably clear. Could see bottom gravel at four fathoms. No crabs on the kelp beds.

By mid-November, the script began to widen, the pen digging deeper into the fiber of the page:

Nov 14. The cistern water has turned hard. Boiled two gallons for tea; left a residue like fine flour, but bitter on the tongue. Checked roof catchment. No sea spray reached the guttering. Where is the mineral coming from?

Nov 21. It does not dry like sea salt. When sea salt dries, it forms cubes. This dries into needles. Long, sharp needles like powdered glass. It collects along the baseboards while I sleep.

Julian paused. He raised the candle and glanced at the base of the wall behind the stove.

A faint, pale line ran the entire perimeter of the chamber. In the dim light, it looked like chalk dust. He stood, knelt beside the stonework, and scraped a fingernail along the skirting.

It came away with a cluster of microscopic, transparent filaments that sparkled under the candle flame. When he touched the tip of his finger to his tongue, there was no saline sting—only an intense, cold numbness that spread instantly across his gums.

He returned to the desk and turned the page.

Dec 02. I heard the bell buoy at South Shoal chime six times between two and three in the morning. The South Shoal buoy was dragged under and lost in the gale of ’98. There is no buoy.

Something is displacing the water from beneath.

The final entry occupied an entire page, written in jagged charcoal:

Dec 09. It is not rising up the stairs. It is condensing out of the air. Keep your mouth closed when the lantern goes out.
